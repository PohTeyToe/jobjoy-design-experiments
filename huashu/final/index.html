This report is set to a 14.5-point baseline grid on US-Letter stock. The measure of the running text holds between sixty and sixty-eight characters per line, and the heading scale steps by a perfect fourth of 1.333. No rule is drawn that is not structural to the reading of the page.

§ 01   Orientation

How to read this report

Each of us is born with natural talents and abilities. When we honour and make use of these talents, we feel good about ourselves. When we do not, we feel unfulfilled. By identifying what motivates us, what our talents are, and how to employ them, we can target work and careers with which we are compatible and experience “JobJoy”.

Your JobJoy Report, which identifies your Key Success Factors, is a tool to help you find a deep sense of job satisfaction. It starts with a definition of your Natural Talents, then describes the key elements that trigger your talents: Preferred Subject Matter, Motivating Situations, Natural Relating Style. These four facets come together to serve your Essential Motivation — the essence of your right work when you are doing what you enjoy most and doing it well.

Using the facts, people and events of your stories, I build a case toward these conclusions. I provide a Personal Statement in which I re-tell your story with the view of painting a more accurate word picture of who you are in terms of your right work. The numbers in your statement correspond to definitions of your Key Success Factors. This builds a strong vocabulary for you to communicate with clarity and confidence to employers the job duties and conditions that make you a motivated and productive employee.

On reading your report, you may find some things resonate well while others do not. Make note of these and send any comments. Many people get the best use of the report after reading it several times. Careful reflection and validation of its contents takes time. Enjoy your report.

§ 02   Elements — definitions

The six elements, defined.

01

Natural Talents. What do you tend to do naturally and easily, that you find yourself doing almost without thinking or planning to do it, and that you always enjoy? You could call these your natural talents. There are many different kinds of natural talents — related to learning, evaluating, organizing, planning, communicating, influencing, observing, doing, and developing. When you use your natural talents as the basis for your job, your job becomes a more enjoyable part of your life, and your life becomes a more productive expression of who you already are.

02

Preferred Subject Matter. What and whom do you enjoy working with the most? Your Preferred Subject Matter is the particular combination of people, information, and modalities that you are most happy to deal with, and which in turn motivates you to do your best work. For some, it is tangible things; for others, it is ideas, numbers, or the lives of other people.

03

Motivating Situations. The situations that turn you into a motivated person, or the conditions that stimulate you to achieve: the circumstances that trigger your involvement, how the activity is structured, what job circumstances stimulate you, what kind of recognition motivates you, and what end use or result makes the effort feel worthwhile.

04

Natural Relating Style. The way in which you prefer to relate to others as you use your Natural Talents to accomplish your Essential Motivation — your most comfortable way to relate with other people, described in terms of Personality Style, Relationship Style, Relationship Type, natural Role, and how you prefer to be Managed.

05

Essential Motivation. The singular, distinguishing, or characteristic result that drives all the other parts of your pattern. It is the central purpose you seek to achieve through your work, and the pivot around which the other five elements turn.

06

What You Do Next. These are the things you must do to create the unique work that is yours alone to do. Do what you love, and the work and the money will follow in their own time.

§ 04   Personal Statement

Personal Statement.

I am  —  a builder and developer of creative projects.

I like to put together the various parts of a creative project (1). And I love developing skills that facilitate my ability to build creative projects. This natural inclination showed up early in life.

I remember having a vivid imagination and could entertain myself for hours with drawing or building forts. I could draw dinosaurs and played Robin Hood and cowboys. I enjoyed using my hands and imagination to create (2). For example, I built with Lego and Kenex as a kid. I usually followed the directions in the Lego box to build the design on the box. I found it more enjoyable to follow the instructions with Lego but would build creatively whatever I wanted with Kenex (3). I still get a kick out of seeing a finished product result from my creative efforts.

I’ve always been drawn to visual material, including colours, design, and drawing. In grade three I wrote a book about a travelling bear for fun and drew all the pictures. My teacher was so impressed she had it laminated and bound so future kids in her class could read it. In grade five, we had a project to paint a picture of a building. My painting was chosen to be displayed with a select few students from the school district, hung in the mayoral office of Victoria (4).

But I was especially drawn to movies. I made my first movie at six or seven with my dad behind the camera because I was too young to operate it. I told him what to shoot and how to narrate it. It was about a dinosaur attacking a city and we used toys to make it. I made my second movie at eight — “The Wild Adventures of Sheriff Jane” — starring my younger sister, my cousins, my dad, aunt, and grandparents. My grandmother entered it into a local film festival when I was twelve and it won audience favourite.

I didn’t have a script but I knew how I wanted the story to go; I knew what every scene should look like before we shot it. I made all the props like a stagecoach and bank teller bars (5). I got my family to dress up in their costumes and would block out the scene and tell them what to say before I rolled the camera. We shot in scene order so we wouldn’t have to edit. I liked seeing my vision come to life (6).

This was me in action. I love to explore and try new things which go beyond the current limits of my knowledge or experience. It usually leads to some kind of vision or goal for something I want to build or develop. It could be a big thing like a movie or a small thing like a painting easel.

I went on to make many small films through my teens. In grade nine I discovered the Victoria Film Festival had a youth category, and I made at least three short films that would show there over the course of my high school years. I would do most of the creative and production work, including editing, which was my favourite activity (7). I have a knack for developing my creative projects by giving the thing its shape or form.

These early years reveal so much about my motivational pattern. I thrive in an open-ended environment that inspires and encourages creativity, where people focus their energy on producing distinctive and unique objects or ideas (8). I respond to a clearly defined or predetermined end, whether the goal is set by me or others.

I loved making people laugh. I loved the attention and later the praise that came with being funny or clever. I enjoyed being funny on the fly (9). I liked being in front of an audience. I think my natural personality is a performer. I like to make speeches, be on stage, or give presentations of any kind. I like to influence and motivate others. Although I performed in many of my high school’s musicals and plays from grades ten to twelve, my favourite drama project was The Laramie Project in grade eleven. It felt fun to build something with a group, to run lines, to take directors’ notes, to make it as good as we could (10).

My mom put my sister and me in summer camp when we were nine and eleven. I found a community there that would keep me going back until I was old enough to work there (11). I do best when I’m in an environment where there is some freedom to talk and interact and make new friends. But also the outdoors (12). Because I like to do things physically and manually, the outdoors makes it easier to use my physical abilities because it requires them.

The camp experience allowed me to test my mettle even as a young kid. I liked feeling proficient at survival-type activities, like making shelters for overnights and building fires, orienteering, feeling physically challenged (13). I love to develop my knowledge or skill to the point of proficiency, then demonstrate my competency by solving problems, producing results, or advising others.

The summer I was sixteen, we went on a twenty-five-day out-trip: kayaking the Gulf Islands, canoeing the Campbell Lake system, and hiking to the peak of Mt. Elkhorn. We had to rely on each other and often were with each other in very deep and desperate moments, navigating not just the land but our own emotional teenage landscapes (14). I function best within the context of a group, team, or organization that I identify with, or that shares my values or my enthusiasm for a certain experience.

I really do like to explore and break through the existing limits of my experience, and travelling allows for that. Driving to Arizona and back in less than a week with my best friend at twenty-three in 2012 was a highlight for me. It felt like a big adventure, seeing places and things we’d never seen before. It felt like freedom (15).

Maybe I’m usually happiest going solo, completely in charge, making all the decisions. I find myself resisting outside influence, and do not feel comfortable on teams or in partnerships unless I can maintain personal autonomy. I liked the sense of adventure and felt brave to be alone, like I was tough and capable (16). As a creative person, I am resourceful and self-contained and find that creativity increases with freedom.

I’m at another crossroads and feel like I’m being boxed in by social and personal limitations. I need to explore again, recover that sense of adventure, get back to my essential motivation — that sense of freedom that comes with using my talents to explore and build and develop a creative project.
